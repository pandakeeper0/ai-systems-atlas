# Moravec's Paradox

**English:** Moravec's Paradox  
**中文:** 莫拉维克悖论

## Core idea

**Moravec's Paradox** is the observation that tasks humans experience as intellectually difficult—such as formal reasoning or calculation—can be comparatively easy for computers, while abilities humans perform almost effortlessly—such as perception, sensorimotor coordination, and navigating the physical world—can be extraordinarily difficult to reproduce in machines.

The paradox is therefore not simply that some tasks are hard and others are easy. It is that **human subjective difficulty is a poor guide to the computational difficulty of reproducing an ability artificially**.

## Why it happens

A common evolutionary explanation is that perception and motor control are the product of very long periods of biological evolution. Human brains therefore perform enormous amounts of highly optimized computation for these abilities without exposing that computation to conscious awareness.

Abstract reasoning is evolutionarily much newer. Because humans experience its intermediate steps consciously, it can feel more difficult even when the underlying computational problem is easier to formalize and automate.

## Origin

The paradox is named after **Hans Moravec**, a roboticist and AI researcher. Moravec articulated the observation in the 1980s, including in his 1988 book *Mind Children: The Future of Robot and Human Intelligence*.

Closely related observations were also made by AI researchers including **Marvin Minsky** and **Rodney Brooks**, particularly in discussions of perception, robotics, and embodied intelligence.
